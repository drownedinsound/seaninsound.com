Spotify exodus

Leaving Spotify: what we learned

Since September 2025 the podcast has heard from artists who took their music down, and from the people asking what listeners can do instead. These are the lessons, with the episodes they came from.

What the guests told us

  1. Laura Burhenn left Spotify and posted a protest monologue after the Helsing investment.
  2. King Gizzard & The Lizard Wizard and Godspeed You! Black Emperor also took music down.
  3. Individual protest can snowball into a movement.
  4. Qobuz and other alternatives come up.
  5. Artists taking part in their own devaluation is part of the bigger picture.
  6. Kadhja Bonet explains why she removed her music from Spotify.
  7. Alexa Speed digs into the corporate influence and investments behind streaming platforms, including AI spending and Daniel Ek's defence-tech ventures.
  8. Bandcamp, Qobuz and other alternatives are discussed as practical places to listen and pay artists.
  9. The guests imagine a future where creativity and community outweigh convenience and art is valued as social good rather than for algorithmic profit.

And in the bigger picture

  • There wasn't really a song of the summer in 2025.
  • Streaming payouts keep shrinking while platforms post record profits.
  • Spotify's ethics (war tech, ICE ads, Joe Rogan) turned into artist boycotts.
  • Ten million Spotify streams paid roughly £32k.
  • Payouts put artists in direct competition with each other, as when a release lands the same month as Taylor Swift.
  • "Just support artists on Bandcamp" misses who actually owns the rights.

Alternatives get a fair hearing, trade-offs included: one guest points out that telling fans to support artists on Bandcamp misses who actually owns the rights.

A note on Qobuz

The Drowned in Sound Podcast is presented in partnership with Qobuz, which is one of the alternatives discussed. We say that up front. The podcast remains editorially independent: Qobuz is our presenting partner because its values on audio quality, fair pay and putting artists first match the show's editorial position, and the show does not carry advertising from streaming platforms whose economics do not work for artists.
